Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) measures your total monthly debt payments as a percentage of your gross monthly income. Divide all monthly debt payments by your gross monthly income and multiply by 100. If you earn $6,000/month gross and pay $1,800/month in debt obligations, your DTI is 30%. Lenders use DTI to determine whether you can afford additional debt — it is one of the most critical factors in loan approval decisions.

When you apply for a mortgage, car loan, or personal loan, lenders look at two things: your credit score and your DTI. Your credit score tells them how reliably you have paid debts in the past. Your DTI tells them whether you can realistically afford new payments going forward. A perfect credit score cannot overcome a DTI that is too high.

## The DTI Formula

**Front-end DTI (housing ratio):**
```
Front-End DTI = Monthly Housing Costs / Gross Monthly Income × 100
```

**Back-end DTI (total debt ratio):**
```
Back-End DTI = Total Monthly Debt Payments / Gross Monthly Income × 100
```

**Worked example:**

| Monthly Income | $7,500 gross |
|---------------|-------------|
| Proposed mortgage (PITI) | $1,800 |
| Car loan payment | $380 |
| Student loan payment | $210 |
| Minimum credit card payments | $150 |
| **Total monthly debt** | **$2,540** |

```
Front-end DTI = $1,800 / $7,500 = 24%
Back-end DTI  = $2,540 / $7,500 = 33.9%
```

This borrower passes typical conventional mortgage guidelines (back-end DTI under 43–45%).

## What DTI Do Lenders Require?

DTI thresholds vary by loan type:

| Loan Type | Front-End Max | Back-End Max |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| Conventional (Fannie/Freddie) | 28% | 36–45% |
| FHA Loan | 31% | 43–57% (with compensating factors) |
| VA Loan | No fixed limit | 41% guideline |
| USDA Loan | 29% | 41% |
| Personal loan (typical) | N/A | 36–50% |
| Auto loan (typical) | N/A | No strict limit, varies by lender |

**The 28/36 rule:** Traditional mortgage underwriting aimed for a front-end DTI under 28% (housing costs under 28% of income) and a back-end DTI under 36% (all debt under 36% of income). Modern automated underwriting has loosened this — many conventional loans approve back-end DTI up to 45%, and FHA loans with strong compensating factors (high credit score, large down payment) may go higher.

## How DTI Affects Your Interest Rate

Even within the qualifying range, a lower DTI typically gets you better loan terms. Lenders view low DTI as lower default risk, which translates to:
- Lower interest rates in some cases
- Easier approval for larger loan amounts
- More loan options and products available
- Higher likelihood of manual underwriting approval when automated systems decline

Borrowers at 43% DTI may qualify — but borderline cases often pay higher rates than borrowers at 30% DTI with identical credit scores.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What monthly obligations count toward DTI?**
For DTI calculation, include: minimum credit card payments (not full balance, just minimum), auto loans, student loans, personal loans, child support paid, alimony paid, proposed mortgage (PITI), and any other installment debt with remaining payments. Do NOT include: utilities, phone bills, insurance premiums, groceries, gym memberships, streaming subscriptions, taxes withheld, or any expense that is not a formal debt obligation.

**Does self-employment income count for DTI?**
Yes, but lenders verify it differently. For W-2 employees, gross monthly income is straightforward. For self-employed borrowers, lenders typically average two years of net self-employment income from tax returns (after business deductions), which often produces a lower qualifying income than gross revenue. This is why some self-employed borrowers find it harder to qualify — the income lenders count is the IRS-reported net profit, not gross revenue.

**Can I improve my DTI before applying for a mortgage?**
Yes. Two levers: reduce debt or increase income. Paying off a car loan or credit card balance before applying directly reduces monthly obligations and lowers DTI. Even paying down credit card balances to lower required minimums helps. On the income side, adding a part-time job, freelance income, or rental income (with documentation) increases the denominator. Allow 2–3 months for debt payoffs to be reflected in updated credit reports before applying.

**What is the difference between DTI and credit utilization?**
DTI measures monthly payments versus income — a lender qualification metric. Credit utilization measures current credit card balances versus total credit limits — a credit score factor. A person with $0 credit card balance has 0% utilization (helps credit score) but if they have high auto loan and student loan payments, DTI can still be high. Both matter for loan approval, but they measure different things.

**If my DTI is too high, should I pay off installment debt or revolving debt first?**
For reducing DTI specifically, pay off whichever debt has the highest monthly payment relative to remaining balance. Paying off a $5,000 car loan with $350/month remaining payment improves DTI more than paying down $5,000 of a $20,000 student loan with $180/month minimum. For credit score improvement, reduce revolving (credit card) balances to below 30% utilization first. Often the best combined strategy: eliminate auto loans and personal loans first, then credit card balances.
