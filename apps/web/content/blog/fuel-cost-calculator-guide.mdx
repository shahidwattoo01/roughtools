## What Is a Fuel Cost Calculator?

A fuel cost calculator is a tool that estimates how much money you will spend on gasoline or diesel for any trip, commute, or time period, based on the distance traveled, your vehicle's fuel economy (in MPG or L/100km), and the current fuel price.

Fuel is typically the second or third largest variable expense for vehicle owners after insurance. Unlike insurance or car payments, fuel cost scales directly with how much you drive — which means it responds immediately to changes in driving behavior, route efficiency, and vehicle choice. Even a modest improvement in fuel economy translates to meaningful annual savings.

The fuel cost formula is simple: divide the distance by MPG to get gallons used, then multiply by the price per gallon. Cost per mile equals price per gallon divided by MPG — a useful figure for comparing total transportation costs across different vehicles or modes.

A fuel cost calculator extends this to practical planning: road trip budgeting, annual commute cost estimation, side-by-side vehicle comparison (how much would you save per year switching from a 22 MPG SUV to a 35 MPG sedan?), and breaking down a vehicle's true cost of ownership beyond the sticker price. It also handles metric conversions (MPG to L/100km) for international vehicles and travel.

## The Fuel Cost Formula

```
Gallons Used = Distance (miles) / MPG
Fuel Cost    = Gallons Used × Price per Gallon

Cost per Mile = Price per Gallon / MPG
```

**Converting MPG to L/100km (for international vehicles):**
```
L/100km = 235.214 / MPG
MPG = 235.214 / (L/100km)
```

Examples:
- 30 MPG = 7.84 L/100km
- 50 MPG = 4.70 L/100km
- 15 MPG = 15.68 L/100km

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I find my car's actual MPG?**
Fill the tank completely; reset your trip odometer. Drive normally until you need fuel again. Fill up completely again and record the gallons added and miles driven. Actual MPG = Miles Driven / Gallons Added. Repeat this process 3–5 times and average the results for an accurate real-world MPG. Your actual MPG may differ from EPA estimates by 10–20% depending on driving conditions (city vs. highway, driving style, weather, load).

**Does driving speed affect fuel economy?**
Significantly. Most vehicles get optimal fuel economy between 45–60 mph. At highway speeds, aerodynamic drag increases with the square of speed — driving at 80 mph burns approximately 20–30% more fuel than driving at 60 mph. Rapid acceleration and hard braking also reduce economy by 15–40% compared to smooth, anticipatory driving. Cruise control on the highway maintains constant speed and typically improves MPG by 7–14% vs. manual speed variation.

**Does the type of fuel matter?**
For standard gasoline vehicles: regular (87 octane) and premium (91+ octane) have the same energy content per gallon. Use whatever octane your manufacturer recommends — most modern engines are designed for regular and will not benefit from premium. Using premium in a regular-car is wasting money ($0.40–0.80 premium per gallon). Performance and luxury vehicles that specifically require premium (not just recommend it) may see engine damage from using regular over time. Never use E85 (85% ethanol) in a vehicle not designed for flex-fuel — it can damage the fuel system.

**How does weather affect fuel economy?**
Cold weather significantly reduces fuel economy: gasoline engines can get 15–25% worse MPG in winter (below 20°F) than in summer. Reasons: cold engines run less efficiently until warmed up; cold air is denser (more aerodynamic drag); oil is thicker; tire pressure drops (underinflated tires increase rolling resistance). EV ranges drop more dramatically in cold weather — batteries lose 10–40% capacity below freezing. Summer heat increases AC load, offsetting some warming benefit to the engine.

**How much does tire pressure affect fuel economy?**
The EPA estimates that properly inflated tires improve gas mileage by 0.5–3%. Each tire underinflated by 10 PSI below recommended reduces fuel economy by approximately 2%. Check tire pressure monthly (especially in temperature changes — tire pressure drops 1 PSI for every 10°F drop in temperature). Properly inflated tires also improve handling, braking, and tire longevity.
