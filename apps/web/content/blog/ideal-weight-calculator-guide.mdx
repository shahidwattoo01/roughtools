Ideal body weight formulas calculate a target weight based on height, sex, and sometimes frame size. The Robinson Formula gives: for men, 52 kg + 1.9 kg per inch over 5 feet; for women, 49 kg + 1.7 kg per inch over 5 feet. A 5'10" (70 inches) man has an ideal weight of approximately 52 + (1.9 × 10) = 71 kg (157 lbs). But this is one of four major formulas — and they can vary by 10–20 lbs.

"Ideal weight" is a concept that evolved from life insurance mortality tables in the 1950s, not from rigorous health science. The single most important truth about body weight is that health is not determined by hitting a specific number on the scale. Body composition (ratio of muscle to fat), fitness level, metabolic health markers, and lifestyle factors predict health outcomes far more reliably than any ideal weight formula.

## How to Use the Ideal Weight Calculator

1. **Enter your height.** In feet and inches or centimeters.
2. **Select your sex.** Formulas differ by biological sex due to average body composition differences.
3. **Select your frame size (optional).** Small, medium, or large frame. Wrist circumference is the most common measurement: for men, wrist ≤6.5" = small frame; 6.5"–7.5" = medium; ≥7.5" = large. For women, wrist ≤5.5" = small; 5.5"–6.5" = medium; ≥6.5" = large.
4. **Read the results.** Ideal weight from four formulas (Robinson, Miller, Hamwi, Devine), the healthy BMI weight range for your height, and a recommended target range that accounts for frame size.

## The Four Major Ideal Weight Formulas

**Devine Formula (1974, originally for drug dosing):**
- Men: 50.0 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet
- Women: 45.5 kg + 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet

**Robinson Formula (1983):**
- Men: 52.0 kg + 1.9 kg per inch over 5 feet
- Women: 49.0 kg + 1.7 kg per inch over 5 feet

**Miller Formula (1983):**
- Men: 56.2 kg + 1.41 kg per inch over 5 feet
- Women: 53.1 kg + 1.36 kg per inch over 5 feet

**Hamwi Formula (1964, for clinical use):**
- Men: 106 lbs + 6 lbs per inch over 5 feet
- Women: 100 lbs + 5 lbs per inch over 5 feet

**Comparison for a 5'8" (68") man (8 inches over 5 feet):**

| Formula | Ideal Weight |
|---------|-------------|
| Devine | 50 + (2.3 × 8) = 68.4 kg = 151 lbs |
| Robinson | 52 + (1.9 × 8) = 67.2 kg = 148 lbs |
| Miller | 56.2 + (1.41 × 8) = 67.5 kg = 149 lbs |
| Hamwi | 106 + (6 × 8) = 154 lbs |
| **BMI Normal Range** | **128–169 lbs (BMI 19.5–25.9)** |

The formulas cluster around 148–154 lbs for a 5'8" man — a 6-lb spread. The healthy BMI weight range is far wider (128–169 lbs), reflecting the real biological variation in what constitutes a healthy weight for someone of that height.

## Why Ideal Weight Is Less Useful Than Body Composition

Consider two 5'8" men both weighing 165 lbs:
- Man A: 12% body fat, significant muscle mass, exercises regularly, excellent metabolic markers
- Man B: 28% body fat, limited muscle mass, sedentary, elevated blood glucose and triglycerides

Man A is above most "ideal weight" estimates but has excellent health. Man B is at a weight that might not trigger concern by any weight chart but has poor metabolic health. The scale tells you nothing about the composition of the weight.

**A better approach:** Instead of targeting a specific weight number, focus on:
1. **Body fat percentage within healthy range** (see body fat calculator)
2. **Waist circumference under 40" for men, 35" for women** (reduces visceral fat risk)
3. **Cardiorespiratory fitness** (can you exercise at moderate intensity for 30+ minutes?)
4. **Metabolic health markers** (blood pressure, fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL)

If these are all in healthy ranges, your weight is appropriate — regardless of what any formula says.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does frame size affect ideal weight?**
Frame size adjustments are typically ±10% of the base calculated ideal weight. For a person with a calculated ideal weight of 150 lbs: small frame target might be 135 lbs; large frame target might be 165 lbs. These adjustments reflect that larger frame individuals genuinely have more bone and structural mass, making a higher weight appropriate.

**Is ideal weight different for athletes?**
Yes. Athletes, especially those with significant muscle development, will typically exceed most ideal weight formula estimates while being extremely healthy. A male powerlifter at 5'10" and 220 lbs would be classified as "obese" by BMI and well above ideal weight formulas — but if body fat percentage is 12%, he is likely metabolically healthier than average despite the elevated scale weight. Ideal weight formulas were not developed for athletes.

**What is the healthiest weight to try to maintain?**
The healthiest weight for you as an individual is the weight at which: your metabolic health markers are in healthy ranges, you can maintain adequate physical function and energy for daily activities and exercise, your body fat percentage is within the healthy range for your age and sex, and you can sustain the lifestyle required to maintain it. For most people, this aligns with the upper half of the normal BMI range (22–25), adjusted for frame and muscle mass.

**How much weight loss is too much?**
Red flags for excessive weight loss: losing more than 1–2 lbs/week consistently (suggesting muscle loss alongside fat), hair loss, constant fatigue, inability to concentrate, menstrual irregularities (women), or feeling cold constantly. These signal that the caloric deficit is too aggressive or that weight loss is occurring from pathological causes (thyroid disease, cancer, eating disorders). Any unintentional weight loss of 5%+ of body weight over 6–12 months warrants medical evaluation.

**Does muscle weigh more than fat?**
Muscle is more dense than fat, not heavier in isolation — a pound of muscle and a pound of fat both weigh one pound. But because muscle is denser, one pound of muscle takes up much less space than one pound of fat. A person who gains muscle while losing fat may see the scale stay flat (or even increase) while their body visibly shrinks, clothing fits differently, and body composition improves significantly. This is why "the scale is not the only measure of progress" is legitimate advice.
