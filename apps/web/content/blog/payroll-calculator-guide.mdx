## What Is a Payroll Calculator?

A payroll calculator is a tool that computes an employee's net pay (take-home pay) from their gross wages, accounting for every required tax withholding and deduction. It is used by small business owners, HR professionals, and accountants to confirm that paycheck amounts are correct before funds are disbursed.

The calculation starts with gross pay — the employee's total compensation before deductions, whether salaried or hourly. From gross pay, the calculator subtracts pre-tax deductions such as health insurance premiums and traditional 401(k) contributions, which reduce taxable income. It then applies FICA taxes (Social Security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%), federal income tax withholding based on the employee's W-4, and state income tax based on the employee's state of residence.

Payroll calculations also account for employer-side obligations that are separate from the employee's pay. Employers must match FICA taxes dollar-for-dollar and pay federal and state unemployment taxes. These are not deducted from the employee's paycheck but represent an additional cost to the employer on top of gross wages.

Getting payroll right is critical. The IRS imposes failure-to-deposit penalties of 2–15% on late or incorrect payroll tax deposits. The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty can hold business owners personally liable for 100% of uncollected payroll taxes — one of the few liabilities that can pierce corporate protection. A payroll calculator helps ensure every number is accurate before funds are released.

## Payroll Calculation Steps

**Step 1: Calculate Gross Pay**
- Salaried employees: Annual salary ÷ number of pay periods (26 for bi-weekly, 24 for semi-monthly, 12 for monthly)
- Hourly employees: Regular hours × hourly rate + overtime hours × (hourly rate × 1.5)

**Step 2: Subtract Pre-Tax Deductions**
These reduce taxable income:
- Employee health insurance premium share
- Traditional 401(k) contributions (up to $23,000/year in 2024)
- HSA contributions
- Flexible Spending Account (FSA)
- Dependent care FSA

Taxable Wages = Gross Pay − Pre-Tax Deductions

**Step 3: Calculate FICA Taxes**
- Social Security: 6.2% on taxable wages (up to Social Security wage base: $168,600 for 2024)
- Medicare: 1.45% on all taxable wages (no wage base)
- Additional Medicare Tax: 0.9% on wages above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (married)

**Step 4: Calculate Federal Income Tax Withholding**
Based on W-4 filing status and withholding elections. Use IRS Publication 15-T withholding tables or percentage method.

**Step 5: Calculate State Income Tax Withholding**
Varies by state. No state income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, Wyoming.

**Step 6: Subtract Post-Tax Deductions**
- Roth 401(k) contributions (no pre-tax benefit, but tax-free growth)
- Life insurance over $50,000 face value
- Garnishments (child support, tax levies)

**Net Pay = Taxable Wages − FICA − Federal Tax − State Tax − Post-Tax Deductions**

## Employer Payroll Taxes (Not Employee Cost)

Employers pay matching FICA taxes plus FUTA/SUTA:
- Employer Social Security: 6.2% (same as employee share)
- Employer Medicare: 1.45%
- FUTA (Federal Unemployment): 6% on first $7,000 (most employers pay effective 0.6% after state credit)
- SUTA (State Unemployment): Varies by state and employer history (0.1%–8%+)

Total employer cost per employee is typically 7–10% above gross wages when adding employer FICA + unemployment taxes + workers' compensation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between gross pay and net pay?**
Gross pay is total compensation before any deductions — the "sticker price" of the job. Net pay is what the employee actually receives after all taxes and deductions. The gap between gross and net can be 20–35% for middle-income earners, which is why a $70,000 salary doesn't equal $70,000 in annual take-home. Most employees receive approximately 65–75% of gross pay as net take-home.

**How do I determine federal withholding without using the IRS tables?**
The Percentage Method (IRS Publication 15-T, Worksheet 1): Multiply gross wages by number of pay periods to get annualized wages; subtract standard deduction ($14,600 for single filer in 2024); calculate tax using 2024 brackets (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%...); divide by pay periods for per-paycheck withholding amount; adjust for any amounts on W-4 Lines 3 and 4. **What happens if I withhold too little payroll tax?**
The employer (business) is responsible for depositing correct withholding taxes to the IRS on time. Underpayment results in IRS penalties: failure-to-deposit penalty of 2–15% of the underpaid amount depending on how late. The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty can hold owners and responsible officers personally liable for 100% of uncollected/undeposited employment taxes — one of the few business tax liabilities that can pierce corporate protection.

**How do pre-tax 401(k) contributions affect payroll?**
Traditional 401(k) contributions are taken from gross pay before calculating federal and state income tax withholding. They do NOT reduce FICA (Social Security and Medicare) taxes — those are calculated on gross compensation including 401(k) contributions. So a $400/paycheck traditional 401(k) contribution saves approximately $400 × 22% (federal marginal rate) = $88 in federal tax per paycheck, while FICA is unchanged. The total immediate tax savings from a traditional 401(k) contribution is your marginal income tax rate × contribution amount.

**When must employers deposit payroll taxes?**
Deposit schedule depends on your deposit liability size: Semi-weekly depositors: must deposit by Wednesday for payrolls paid Sat–Tue; by Friday for payrolls paid Wed–Fri. Monthly depositors: deposit by the 15th of the following month. New employers start on a monthly schedule. Deposits are made electronically through EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System). IRS Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Tax Return) reports quarterly withholding; Form 944 is an annual alternative for small employers.
