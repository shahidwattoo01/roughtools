The minimum recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for protein is 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight (0.36 g/lb) per day. For a 70 kg (154 lb) person: 70 × 0.8 = **56 grams per day** minimum. However, for active individuals and those seeking muscle growth, research strongly supports higher intake: 1.6–2.2 g/kg (0.73–1.0 g/lb) per day for strength training athletes; 1.2–1.6 g/kg for endurance athletes; 1.2–2.0 g/kg for older adults (50+) preserving muscle mass.

The RDA represents the minimum to prevent deficiency — not the optimal amount for performance, body composition, or muscle preservation. The fitness and sports nutrition research consistently shows benefits from protein intake 2–3× the RDA for most physically active people, particularly those in calorie restriction (dieting) or strength training.

## Protein Requirements by Goal

| Goal | Protein (g/kg body weight) | For 70kg person |
|------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| Minimum (RDA) | 0.8 g/kg | 56g/day |
| General health (sedentary) | 0.8–1.0 g/kg | 56–70g/day |
| Endurance sports | 1.2–1.6 g/kg | 84–112g/day |
| Muscle building | 1.6–2.2 g/kg | 112–154g/day |
| Strength/power athletes | 1.8–2.4 g/kg | 126–168g/day |
| Weight loss (muscle preservation) | 1.8–2.6 g/kg | 126–182g/day |
| Older adults (50+) | 1.2–2.0 g/kg | 84–140g/day |

Higher protein during calorie restriction preserves lean muscle mass — a key finding in research. When cutting calories, getting 2.0–2.4 g/kg of protein dramatically reduces muscle loss compared to the RDA amount.

## Timing: Does "Anabolic Window" Matter?

Research on protein timing has evolved. Current understanding:
- **Total daily intake** is the primary determinant of muscle protein synthesis — not timing
- **Even distribution** across 3–5 meals appears slightly superior to front-loading or back-loading
- **Pre/post-workout protein** is beneficial but not as critical as previously thought ("anabolic window" extends to 4–6 hours, not 30 minutes)
- **Pre-sleep protein** (slow-digesting casein: cottage cheese, Greek yogurt) may enhance overnight muscle protein synthesis

Practical guidance: spread protein evenly across meals, aim for 0.25–0.40 g/kg per meal, consume protein within a few hours of training.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can you eat too much protein?**
For healthy adults, high protein intake (up to 3.5 g/kg) appears safe and is not associated with kidney damage in people without pre-existing kidney disease. The concern about protein damaging kidneys applies specifically to those with chronic kidney disease (CKD), for whom protein restriction is medically advised. For healthy individuals: excess protein is either used for energy or excreted; it does not accumulate harmfully. Extremely high intakes (4+ g/kg) provide no additional benefit and are unnecessary and expensive.

**Is plant protein as good as animal protein?**
Plant proteins are generally lower in bioavailability (the percentage your body can use) compared to animal proteins — plant protein scores 65–85% vs. 90–97% for animal protein on PDCAAS (Protein Digestibility Corrected Amino Acid Score). Some plant foods are incomplete (lacking one or more essential amino acids). However, eating varied plant protein sources (legumes + grains, for example) provides all essential amino acids. Vegans and vegetarians should aim for the higher end of protein recommendations (by 10–25%) to compensate for lower bioavailability.

**Does protein make you fat?**
Protein is the most satiating macronutrient per calorie — high-protein diets are associated with lower total calorie intake. Protein has a higher thermic effect (20–30% of its calories are used in digestion) vs. carbohydrates (5–10%) and fat (0–3%). Excess calories from any source cause weight gain. But gram-for-gram, protein is the least likely macronutrient to contribute to fat gain because of satiety, thermic effect, and its use in muscle repair/synthesis.

**What is the "leucine threshold" for muscle protein synthesis?**
Leucine is a branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) that acts as the primary trigger for muscle protein synthesis (MPS). Each meal needs to reach a leucine threshold (~2–3g) to maximally stimulate MPS. High-quality animal proteins (meat, dairy, eggs) naturally provide enough leucine in a normal serving. Plant proteins often have lower leucine content — plant-based athletes may benefit from leucine supplementation or choosing leucine-rich plant foods (soy, spirulina).

**Should I supplement with protein powder?**
Protein supplements (whey, casein, plant-based) are convenient and effective ways to hit protein targets, but they're supplements to diet, not replacements. Food sources provide protein plus micronutrients, fiber (in plant sources), and satiety benefits. If you consistently fall short of protein goals through food alone, supplementation makes sense. Whey protein is the most studied and rapidly absorbed — ideal post-workout. Casein (slow-digesting) works well before sleep. Plant protein blends (pea + rice) are effective alternatives for vegans and those with dairy sensitivities.
