Return on Investment (ROI) = (Net Profit / Cost of Investment) × 100. If you invest $5,000 and receive $6,500 back, your net profit = $1,500, and ROI = ($1,500 / $5,000) × 100 = **30%**. For investments over multiple years, always calculate annualized ROI using CAGR so you can compare investments of different durations: a 30% total ROI over 2 years ≠ 30% annual ROI.

ROI is the most widely used metric for evaluating the profitability of decisions — from business investments and marketing campaigns to real estate purchases and education. Its simplicity (a single percentage) makes it easy to compare disparate investments, though that simplicity also hides important context (risk, time horizon, opportunity cost).

## ROI Formula Variations

**Simple ROI (single period or total):**
```
ROI = (Ending Value - Beginning Value) / Beginning Value × 100
    = Net Profit / Investment Cost × 100
```

**Annualized ROI (for multi-year comparisons):**
```
Annualized ROI = [(1 + ROI/100)^(1/years) - 1] × 100
```

A 60% total ROI over 4 years: Annualized = [(1.60)^(1/4) − 1] × 100 = 12.47% per year.

**ROI with ongoing costs:**
```
Net ROI = (Total Returns - Total Costs) / Total Costs × 100
```

Include all costs: initial purchase, maintenance, taxes, fees, transaction costs.

## ROI Examples Across Contexts

**Marketing Campaign:**
- Spend: $20,000 on Google Ads
- Revenue attributed: $65,000
- Cost of goods (50%): $32,500
- Net profit: $65,000 − $32,500 − $20,000 = $12,500
- ROI = $12,500 / $20,000 = **62.5%**

**Real Estate:**
- Property purchase: $200,000
- Rental income (1 year): $24,000
- Expenses (taxes, maintenance, insurance): $8,000
- Net income: $16,000
- ROI = $16,000 / $200,000 = **8% annual**

**Education (MBA):**
- Total cost (tuition, lost income, fees): $200,000
- Annual salary increase post-MBA: $30,000
- Simple payback period: 200,000 / 30,000 = **6.7 years**
- 10-year ROI: ($30,000 × 10 − $200,000) / $200,000 = **50% over 10 years**

## What Is a Good ROI?

"Good" ROI is context-dependent and must exceed opportunity cost:

| Investment Type | Expected ROI Range | Notes |
|---------------|-------------------|-------|
| Savings account (HYSA) | 4–5% annually | Baseline risk-free |
| S&P 500 stocks | 10–11% annually (historical) | Moderate volatility |
| Real estate | 6–12% annually | Location-dependent |
| Business investment | 15–30%+ | Depends on risk |
| Marketing (digital) | 100–500% | High variance |
| Startup investment | −100% to 1,000%+ | Mostly negative; some huge wins |

A 10% ROI on a real estate investment may be excellent; a 10% ROI on a marketing campaign may be terrible if risk-free alternatives offer 4–5%.

## Common ROI Calculation Mistakes

**Ignoring time value:** 100% ROI over 10 years = 7.18% per year. 100% ROI over 1 year = 100%. They're not comparable. Always annualize.

**Forgetting all costs:** Real estate ROI calculations often miss property taxes, insurance, vacancy periods, maintenance, and property management fees — which can reduce advertised "8%" returns to 3%.

**Confusing revenue with profit:** Marketing ROI should use gross profit (revenue minus cost of goods), not revenue. Using revenue overstates ROI significantly in low-margin businesses.

**Not accounting for risk:** Two investments with identical ROI are not equivalent if one has much higher risk. Risk-adjusted ROI (Sharpe ratio for investments) accounts for this.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a 5:1 ROI and is it good?**
A 5:1 ROI means for every $1 invested, you receive $5 in return — a 400% ROI (($5 − $1) / $1 = 400%). In marketing, a 5:1 return on ad spend (ROAS) is often cited as a minimum healthy threshold. However, whether this is "good" depends on your margins: a 50% gross margin business with 5:1 ROAS earns $2.50 profit per dollar spent on ads — strong. A 20% gross margin business with 5:1 ROAS earns only $1.00 in gross profit per dollar spent — break-even before overhead.

**How do I calculate social media ROI when attribution is difficult?**
Social media ROI is difficult to measure precisely because the path from content to purchase is often indirect and multi-touch. Common approaches: Last-click attribution (credit the last touchpoint before purchase — undervalues social's role); UTM parameters on links to track social-driven traffic and conversions in Google Analytics; promo codes unique to social campaigns; lift studies (compare purchase rates in exposed vs. unexposed audiences). For brand awareness campaigns, track proxy metrics: reach, engagement rates, and surveys about brand awareness changes.

**How do I calculate ROI on an employee?**
Employee ROI = Value Generated / Total Compensation Cost. Total compensation = salary + benefits + employer taxes + overhead allocation. A salesperson earning $80,000 with $25,000 in overhead and benefits ($105,000 total cost) who generates $400,000 in gross margin: ROI = ($400,000 − $105,000) / $105,000 = 281%. This is revenue-generating roles. For support roles, ROI is calculated differently — measuring cost savings, quality improvements, or capacity enablement rather than direct revenue.

**Is ROI the same as rate of return?**
"Return on investment" (ROI) and "rate of return" are often used interchangeably, but technically differ. Rate of return is typically annualized and usually refers to financial investments. ROI may refer to total return over the entire period (not annualized), and is used more broadly in business contexts (marketing ROI, employee ROI). When comparing investments with different time horizons, always use annualized rate of return (CAGR) rather than simple ROI percentage.

**What discount rate should I use to compare investments?**
Your discount rate (hurdle rate) should reflect the return you could earn on the next-best alternative. For personal investment decisions: your expected stock market return (7–10%) if you would otherwise invest in stocks; or your debt interest rate if you would otherwise use that money to pay down debt. For businesses: WACC (weighted average cost of capital) — typically 8–15% for established companies. Projects with ROI above the hurdle rate create value; below it, they destroy value relative to alternatives.
