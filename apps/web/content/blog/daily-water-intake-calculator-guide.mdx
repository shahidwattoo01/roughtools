Your daily water intake needs depend on your body weight, activity level, and environment. A general starting formula: drink 0.5–1 ounce of water per pound of body weight daily. A 160-pound moderately active person needs 80–160 ounces (2.4–4.7 liters) per day. Add 12 ounces for every 30 minutes of exercise and more in hot climates.

The classic "8 glasses a day" (eight 8-ounce glasses = 64 oz = 1.9 liters) rule has no strong scientific backing as a universal recommendation. Optimal hydration is highly individual — it depends on body size, activity, sweat rate, diet (foods contribute 20% of daily water intake), climate, and health conditions. The most reliable indicator of hydration is urine color: pale yellow indicates good hydration; dark yellow to amber indicates dehydration.

## The Water Intake Formula

**Base hydration formula:**
```
Daily Water (oz) = Body Weight (lbs) × 0.67
```

**Adjusted for activity:**
```
Add 12 oz for every 30 minutes of moderate exercise
Add 16–24 oz for every 30 minutes of intense exercise or excessive sweating
```

**Worked example — 175-lb person, 45 minutes daily exercise:**
```
Base: 175 × 0.67 = 117 oz
Exercise addition: 18 oz (45 min moderate exercise)
Total: 135 oz ≈ 4.0 liters per day
```

**National Academies of Medicine total water recommendations** (includes water from all beverages and food):
- Men: 3.7 liters/day (125 oz) total water
- Women: 2.7 liters/day (91 oz) total water

Since food provides approximately 20% of total water intake, the daily beverage target is:
- Men: ~3.0 liters from beverages (101 oz)
- Women: ~2.2 liters from beverages (74 oz)

## Signs of Dehydration

Dehydration begins affecting performance at 1–2% body weight fluid loss — before thirst becomes pronounced:

| Dehydration Level | Symptoms |
|-------------------|---------|
| Mild (1–2% BW) | Thirst, slightly reduced focus, darker urine |
| Moderate (3–5% BW) | Headache, reduced physical performance, fatigue, dry mouth |
| Severe (>5% BW) | Dizziness, confusion, rapid heartbeat, heat exhaustion risk |

**Urine color hydration guide:**
- Pale straw/light yellow: well hydrated
- Bright yellow: normal, possibly slightly under-hydrated
- Dark yellow: dehydrated — drink more
- Amber/orange: significantly dehydrated
- Clear: potentially over-hydrated (rare but possible with excessive water intake)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does coffee count toward daily water intake?**
Yes. Despite the common belief that caffeine causes significant dehydration, research shows that caffeinated beverages contribute to daily fluid intake, not against it. Caffeine has a mild diuretic effect, but the water content of coffee and tea more than compensates. A cup of coffee counts as approximately 80–90% of its volume toward hydration. However, very large amounts of caffeine (>500 mg/day) can increase urine output sufficiently to require additional water.

**Can you drink too much water?**
Yes. Hyponatremia (water intoxication) occurs when excessive water intake dilutes sodium in the blood to dangerous levels. It is most common in endurance athletes who drink large amounts of plain water during prolonged exercise without electrolyte replacement. Symptoms include nausea, headache, confusion, and in severe cases, seizures. For most people consuming normal amounts, the kidneys can excrete up to one liter of excess water per hour, making overhydration from casual drinking very unlikely.

**How does hydration affect weight and the scale?**
Hydration status affects scale weight by 2–5 pounds (1–2 kg) on any given day. After intense exercise, the scale may show lower weight due to sweat fluid loss — this is temporary water weight, not fat loss. After high-carbohydrate meals, the scale may show higher weight because carbohydrates bind water (each gram of glycogen stores approximately 3 grams of water). For accurate body weight trends, weigh yourself at the same time each day (morning, after using the bathroom) and track weekly averages.

**Do I need electrolytes or just water?**
For daily hydration and exercise under 60 minutes, plain water is sufficient for most people. For exercise lasting over 60–90 minutes, high-intensity training in heat, or any activity with significant sweating, electrolyte replacement becomes important. Sodium (primary electrolyte in sweat) and potassium are the most critical. Sports drinks, electrolyte tablets, or salty foods can replace what is lost. Over-hydrating with plain water without electrolyte replacement during prolonged endurance exercise is when hyponatremia risk increases.

**Should I drink more water to lose weight?**
Drinking water before meals is associated with modest weight loss in some studies — likely by increasing fullness and reducing caloric intake. Replacing caloric beverages (soda, juice, alcohol) with water reduces caloric intake directly. Water also has zero calories, increases metabolism slightly (particularly if cold, as the body warms it), and supports the metabolic processes involved in fat burning. However, water is not a fat-loss mechanism on its own — it supports fat loss when combined with appropriate caloric deficit.
