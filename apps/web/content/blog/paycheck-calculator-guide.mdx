Your take-home pay (net pay) is your gross salary minus all federal taxes, state taxes, FICA (Social Security and Medicare), and any voluntary deductions. On a $60,000 salary paid semi-monthly, your gross per-paycheck is $2,500. After federal taxes, Social Security, Medicare, and a typical state income tax, take-home pay is typically $1,900–$2,050 per paycheck — a 20–24% reduction.

Most employees know their salary but have never actually calculated whether their paycheck stub makes sense. Understanding what each deduction is — and how to reduce your tax withholding legally — puts you in control of your cash flow and helps you avoid both underpayment penalties and over-withholding (giving the government an interest-free loan).

## How Paycheck Deductions Work

**Gross pay** is your starting number — salary ÷ pay periods, or hourly rate × hours worked.

**Federal income tax** is withheld based on your W-4 filing status and the IRS withholding tables. It is not a flat percentage — it is a marginal tax calculation. For 2024, the brackets are: 10% on income up to $11,600, 12% up to $47,150, 22% up to $100,525, 24% up to $191,950, and higher rates above that (for single filers).

**FICA taxes** are:
- Social Security: 6.2% of gross wages up to $168,600 (2024 wage base)
- Medicare: 1.45% of all wages (plus 0.9% additional Medicare tax on wages above $200,000 for single filers)

**State income tax** depends on your state. Nine states have no income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming.

**Pre-tax deductions** reduce taxable income:
- 401(k) / 403(b) contributions
- Traditional IRA payroll deduction
- Health insurance premiums (employer-sponsored)
- HSA contributions
- Dependent care FSA

**Post-tax deductions** come after taxes:
- Roth 401(k) contributions (post-tax)
- Life insurance premiums
- Wage garnishments
- Union dues

**Worked example — $75,000 salary, single, semi-monthly paycheck:**

| Deduction | Per Paycheck | Annual |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| Gross pay | $3,125.00 | $75,000 |
| Federal income tax | −$468.00 | −$11,232 |
| Social Security (6.2%) | −$193.75 | −$4,650 |
| Medicare (1.45%) | −$45.31 | −$1,087 |
| State tax (CA ~5.5%) | −$171.88 | −$4,125 |
| 401(k) 6% pre-tax | −$187.50 | −$4,500 |
| Health insurance | −$125.00 | −$3,000 |
| **Net take-home** | **$1,933.56** | **$46,405** |

Effective total deduction rate: 38.1% of gross. Take-home pay represents 61.9% of gross salary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between gross and net salary?**
Gross salary is your total compensation before any deductions — what your offer letter says. Net salary (take-home pay) is what you actually receive after all tax withholding and deductions. The gap between them depends on your tax bracket, state, filing status, pre-tax benefits, and voluntary deductions. For most employees, net pay is 65–80% of gross pay.

**Why might my paycheck be different from the calculator estimate?**
Several factors cause variation: overtime or bonuses (taxed at a higher supplemental rate of 22% federally), changes to pay frequency, additional withholding you requested on your W-4, varying health insurance or 401(k) contributions, or mid-year changes to tax bracket thresholds. The calculator provides an estimate; your actual paycheck depends on your employer's specific payroll calculations and any year-to-date adjustments.

**How do I change my withholding amount?**
Submit a new W-4 to your HR or payroll department. The 2020+ W-4 no longer uses "allowances" — it uses a direct dollar approach. If you had a large tax refund last year, you are over-withholding; request less tax withheld by claiming the additional income or reducing additional withholding. If you owed taxes, increase withholding by claiming less or adding an additional dollar amount per paycheck.

**Are bonuses taxed differently?**
Yes. The IRS considers bonuses "supplemental wages." Employers can withhold federal income tax on bonuses at a flat 22% (supplemental wage withholding rate) up to $1 million. For bonuses over $1 million, the rate jumps to 37%. Some employers aggregate the bonus with the regular paycheck and withhold at your effective rate. Either way, state taxes also apply. You may get a partial refund at tax time if the withholding rate was higher than your marginal rate.

**What happens to Social Security tax if I earn more than $168,600?**
The Social Security wage base ($168,600 in 2024) caps how much of your income is subject to the 6.2% Social Security tax. Income above this amount is not subject to Social Security tax — but it is still subject to Medicare (1.45%) and the additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on wages above $200,000. High earners effectively see their FICA burden drop as a percentage of income once they cross the Social Security wage base.
