## What Is a Tax Bracket?

A tax bracket is a range of income taxed at a specific rate within the US federal progressive income tax system. "Progressive" means higher income is taxed at higher rates — but only the portion of income that falls within each bracket's range is taxed at that bracket's rate. Income below the bracket threshold continues to be taxed at the lower rates.

For example, in 2024 a single filer's first $11,600 of taxable income is taxed at 10%, the next $35,550 (income between $11,601 and $47,150) at 12%, and income from $47,151 to $100,525 at 22%. If your taxable income is $60,000, only $12,850 of it (the portion above $47,150) is taxed at 22% — the rest is taxed at lower rates.

Your marginal tax rate is the rate that applies to your next dollar of income — the rate of the highest bracket you have reached. Your effective tax rate is your total tax divided by your total income — always lower than your marginal rate in a progressive system. The gap between these two numbers is the source of the most common tax misunderstanding.

Understanding your actual tax situation helps you estimate take-home pay accurately, evaluate traditional versus Roth retirement account contributions, calculate the real value of tax deductions (a $1,000 deduction saves $220 in federal tax at a 22% marginal rate), and plan around bonuses or additional income. ## Step-by-Step Tax Calculation

**Example: $75,000 gross income, single filer, 2024**

1. AGI = $75,000 (assuming no above-the-line deductions)
2. Standard deduction: $14,600
3. Taxable income: $75,000 − $14,600 = **$60,400**

| Bracket | Income in Bracket | Tax |
|---------|-----------------|-----|
| 10% | $11,600 | $1,160 |
| 12% | $35,550 ($47,150 − $11,600) | $4,266 |
| 22% | $13,250 ($60,400 − $47,150) | $2,915 |
| **Total** | | **$8,341** |

- Marginal tax rate: **22%** (highest bracket reached)
- Effective tax rate: $8,341 / $75,000 = **11.1%** (of gross income)
- Effective tax rate on taxable income: $8,341 / $60,400 = **13.8%**

## Marginal vs. Effective Rate: Why It Matters

**Marginal rate:** The rate on your next dollar of income. If you're at 22% marginal rate, earning an extra $1,000 costs you $220 in federal tax (plus state taxes and FICA).

**Effective rate:** Your total tax divided by total income. If your effective rate is 16%, you keep $0.84 of every dollar earned on average.

**Practical implications:**
- A 22% marginal rate on a $100 bonus = $22 in federal tax, not $22 + taxes on all your other income
- Traditional vs. Roth 401k decision: if marginal rate now is 22% but expected marginal rate in retirement is 12%, Traditional is advantageous (lower taxes later)
- A $10,000 tax deduction saves you: deduction × marginal rate = $10,000 × 22% = **$2,200 in federal taxes**

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a raise ever cost you money due to taxes?**
No — the marginal tax system means you always take home more money after a raise, just at a decreasing rate as income increases. The myth that "getting a raise could put me in a higher bracket and cost me money" is false. Only the income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate. A raise from $47,000 to $50,000 (crossing into the 22% bracket) means only the last $2,850 is taxed at 22%; the first $47,150 continues at 10–12%.

**What is AMT (Alternative Minimum Tax)?**
The Alternative Minimum Tax is a parallel tax system that limits certain deductions and applies a flat rate (26% or 28%) after a large exemption ($85,700 for single filers in 2024). You owe whichever is higher: regular income tax or AMT. The AMT was designed to prevent wealthy individuals from using so many deductions that they owed no tax. After the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017), fewer than 200,000 taxpayers are subject to AMT annually (it used to affect millions).

**What is the SALT deduction cap?**
The State and Local Tax (SALT) deduction allows itemizing taxpayers to deduct state income taxes and/or property taxes. The TCJA (2017) capped this at $10,000 per household ($5,000 if MFS). This particularly affects high-income taxpayers in high-tax states (California, New York, New Jersey) who previously deducted $20,000–$50,000+ in state taxes. The $10,000 cap is set to expire after 2025 unless extended by Congress.

**How do capital gains affect my tax bracket?**
Long-term capital gains (assets held over 1 year) are taxed at special rates: 0% (for incomes up to approximately $47,025 for single filers), 15% (up to $518,900), or 20%. Short-term capital gains (held 1 year or less) are taxed as ordinary income at your regular bracket rates. Capital gains do "stack on top" of ordinary income to determine which bracket applies, but the gains themselves are taxed at preferential rates. The Medicare surtax (3.8%) applies to investment income above $200,000 single / $250,000 MFJ.

**What are "above-the-line" deductions?**
Above-the-line deductions reduce Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) and are available regardless of whether you itemize. Key above-the-line deductions: Traditional IRA contributions (up to $7,000/year in 2024); Student loan interest (up to $2,500); Self-employed health insurance; Half of self-employment tax; HSA contributions. These are particularly valuable because lower AGI also reduces income-based phaseouts for credits, affects eligibility for Roth IRA contributions, and reduces Medicare premium surcharges (IRMAA) for high earners.
