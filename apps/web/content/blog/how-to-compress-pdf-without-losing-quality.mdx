## Compression Level Guide — Screen vs Ebook vs Print

Choosing the wrong compression level is the most common mistake.

**Screen quality (72–96 DPI)**
- Smallest file size possible
- Images look identical on any monitor, phone, or tablet
- Any quality difference is only visible when printed at A3+ size
- **Use for:** email, portal uploads, job applications, government forms, WhatsApp sharing

**Ebook quality (150 DPI)**
- Balanced size and clarity
- Safe for documents that might be printed at A4 size
- **Use for:** archiving, sharing with colleagues, documents that may be printed

**Print quality (300 DPI)**
- Minimal file size reduction (5–15%)
- Preserves full original image resolution
- **Use for:** sending to a print shop, professional printing, architectural drawings

For 95% of everyday use — email, websites, portals — **Screen quality** is always the right choice.

## Why PDF Files Get So Large

**Top 5 causes of oversized PDFs:**

1. **300 DPI embedded images** — PowerPoint and Word export at print resolution by default. A single full-page photo at 300 DPI weighs 3–8 MB. You can compress it to under 300 KB at 96 DPI with zero visible change on screen.

2. **Full embedded font sets** — Each font file adds 200–500 KB. A PDF using 5 different fonts carries 1–2.5 MB in font data alone.

3. **Duplicated content** — A logo on every slide may be stored 40 times instead of once. PDF optimizers detect and remove duplicates.

4. **Uncompressed scanned pages** — Raw TIFF or BMP images inside a PDF inflate size dramatically. Compression reduces them 50–75%.

5. **Hidden metadata and layers** — Design tool PDFs carry editing history, hidden layers, and revision data invisible in the final document. Flattening removes this overhead.

## Tips to Make PDF Smaller Without Quality Loss

- **Delete blank pages and unnecessary pages first.** Use [Split PDF](/tools/pdf/split-pdf) to remove them before compressing — fewer pages means smaller file.
- **Re-export from source at 150 DPI.** If the PDF came from Word or PowerPoint, re-exporting with "150 PPI" image settings produces a smaller file than compressing after export.
- **Use Screen quality for everything digital.** Print quality (300 DPI) barely reduces file size and is only needed if the document will be physically printed at A3 or larger.
- **Don't compress twice at the same level.** The second pass produces near-zero reduction and accumulates image quality loss.
- **Flatten transparent layers first.** In Acrobat or Illustrator, flatten before exporting to remove hidden overhead.
