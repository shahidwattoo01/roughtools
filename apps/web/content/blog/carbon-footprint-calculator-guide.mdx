The average American emits approximately 14–16 metric tons of CO2-equivalent per year — about double the global average of 7 tons and four times the 3.5-ton target needed by 2030 to limit warming to 1.5°C. A personal carbon footprint adds up: driving 15,000 miles per year (~4.6 tons), home energy use (~2.5 tons), diet (~2.1 tons), flights (~1.5 tons per round trip to Europe), and consumer goods (~1.5 tons) combine quickly.

Calculate your personal carbon footprint to understand your impact, identify where your highest emissions come from, and make targeted decisions to reduce them. Not all actions have equal impact — knowing which changes matter most prevents the common mistake of focusing on minor changes (paper straws, reusable bags) while missing major sources (flights, car use, red meat consumption).

## Carbon Footprint Categories

**Transportation (~29% of US average footprint):**
- Personal vehicle driving (most significant for most people)
- Air travel (especially long-haul and business class)
- Public transit, Uber/Lyft rides

**Home energy (~20%):**
- Electricity (depends heavily on your grid's energy mix)
- Natural gas / home heating oil
- The carbon intensity of electricity varies dramatically by state/region

**Diet (~14%):**
- Beef and lamb are highest (20–30 kg CO2 per kg of meat)
- Dairy, pork, poultry are moderate
- Plant-based foods are lowest

**Consumer goods and services (~22%):**
- New products (manufacturing emissions)
- Electronics, clothing, household items
- Services (hotels, restaurants, financial services)

**Other (~15%):**
- Waste and landfill
- Water use
- Medical care

## Highest-Impact Individual Actions

Research from environmental science (Wynes & Nicholas, 2017; Project Drawdown) consistently identifies these as the highest-impact individual actions:

| Action | Annual CO2 Savings |
|--------|------------------|
| Have one fewer child | 58 tons/year (controversial; structural impact) |
| Live car-free | 2.4 tons/year |
| Avoid one transatlantic flight | 1.6 tons/year |
| Switch to plant-rich diet | 0.5–1.5 tons/year |
| Buy 100% renewable electricity | 1.5 tons/year |
| Replace car trip with public transit | 2.2 tons/year average |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a metric ton of CO2 and how big is it?**
One metric ton = 1,000 kilograms = 2,205 pounds. CO2 is a gas, but it's convenient to think of it in mass. At room temperature, 1 metric ton of CO2 fills approximately 529 cubic meters — about the volume of a small house. The average American generates 15 tons of CO2 equivalent per year, equivalent to the volume of roughly 15 small houses worth of CO2.

**Is carbon offsetting effective?**
Carbon offsets (paying for tree planting, renewable energy projects, etc. to "offset" your own emissions) are controversial. Criticisms: many offset projects don't deliver permanent carbon removal (trees die, forests burn); additionality is hard to verify (would the project have happened anyway?); offsets may enable continued high-emission behavior rather than incentivizing reduction. Higher-quality offsets: direct air capture technology, verified permanent storage. Lower-quality: some tree planting, some cookstove projects. The scientific consensus is that offsets are not a substitute for actual emissions reductions but may supplement genuine reduction efforts.

**How much does diet change matter for carbon footprint?**
Diet is one of the highest-leverage personal decisions. The average American diet emits approximately 2.5 tons of CO2 per year; a vegan diet emits approximately 1.0 tons — a difference of 1.5 tons/year. A "flexitarian" diet (reducing meat, especially beef, to once/week) saves approximately 0.5–1.0 tons/year. Eliminating beef specifically (while keeping other meat and dairy) saves about 0.5 tons/year. These are meaningful reductions that compound over time.

**Does my electricity provider affect my carbon footprint?**
Significantly. The US average is 386g CO2 per kWh. States with high renewable penetration: Washington state (hydroelectric-heavy): ~75g/kWh; California: ~198g/kWh. States with high coal: West Virginia: ~810g/kWh; Wyoming: ~850g/kWh. Switching to a green energy plan (100% renewable) from your utility can cut home electricity emissions by 80–95%. Installing solar panels eliminates electricity emissions for self-generated power.

**How do companies calculate their carbon footprint?**
Corporate carbon accounting uses the GHG Protocol standards with three "scopes": Scope 1 = direct emissions (company vehicles, manufacturing); Scope 2 = purchased electricity; Scope 3 = all indirect emissions (supply chain, employee commuting, product use, end-of-life disposal). Scope 3 typically represents 70–90% of a company's total emissions but has historically been least reported. The SEC now requires US public companies to disclose Scope 1, 2, and material Scope 3 emissions.
