## What Is a Discount Calculator?

A discount calculator is a tool that computes the final sale price, the dollar amount saved, and the percentage saved when a product is marked down by a given percentage — or works in reverse to find the original price or the discount percentage when you know the other two values.

Discounts are expressed as a percentage reduction from the original (list) price. A 30% discount on a $100 item means you pay $70 and save $30. The discount amount and sale price are straightforward to compute, but many people struggle with the reverse calculation: if a sale item is priced at $70 and you know the discount is 30%, what was the original price? The answer ($70 ÷ 0.70 = $100) requires dividing by the complement of the discount, which is less intuitive than simple multiplication.

Discount calculations matter in several contexts: as a shopper comparing sale offers, as a retailer setting markdown pricing and evaluating margin impact, when negotiating bulk or trade discounts with suppliers, when stacking multiple discounts (a 20%-off coupon on top of a 30%-off sale is not 50% off — it is 44% off), and when auditing invoices for correct pricing.

A discount calculator handles all three calculation modes — find sale price, find original price, find discount percentage — so you always get the correct figure regardless of which values you know.

## The Discount Formulas

**Sale Price from discount %:**
```
Sale Price = Original Price × (1 − Discount%)
```

**Discount amount:**
```
Discount Amount = Original Price × Discount%
```

**Discount % from prices:**
```
Discount% = ((Original − Sale) / Original) × 100
```

**Original price from sale price:**
```
Original Price = Sale Price / (1 − Discount%)
```

**Worked examples:**

| Original | Discount | Sale Price | Savings |
|----------|---------|-----------|---------|
| $200 | 40% | $120 | $80 |
| $85 | 15% | $72.25 | $12.75 |
| $1,299 | 25% | $974.25 | $324.75 |
| $49.99 | 10% | $44.99 | $5.00 |

**Stacked discounts — 30% off, then 20% off:**
```
$100 × (1 − 0.30) = $70
$70 × (1 − 0.20)  = $56
Combined discount: ($100 − $56) / $100 = 44%
```

Key insight: 30% + 20% ≠ 50% when stacked sequentially. The second discount applies to the already-reduced price. 44% is the correct combined discount.

## Coupon and Sale Combinations

**Two separate coupons:** A 20%-off coupon applied to an item already on sale for 30% off results in 44% total savings (not 50%).

**Dollar-off vs. percentage-off:** Which is better? A "$20 off" coupon vs. "15% off" on a $150 item: $20 off = saves $20; 15% off = saves $22.50. The percentage discount wins here. On a $100 item: $20 off = saves $20; 15% off = saves $15. Dollar-off wins on lower-priced items. Break-even: $20/$item × 100% = item price × 15% → $item = $20/0.15 = $133.33. The dollar-off coupon is better for items under $133.33; percentage-off is better above $133.33.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between a discount and a markdown?**
In retail, both refer to price reductions, but the terms have slightly different connotations. A discount is typically a temporary reduction (a sale, coupon, or promotional offer). A markdown is a permanent price reduction (the item will not return to the original price). Accounting treatments also differ: markdowns reduce the item's carrying value on the balance sheet; temporary discounts are treated as revenue reductions.

**How do I calculate the original price if the sale price and savings amount are known?**
Original Price = Sale Price + Savings Amount. Example: paid $70, saved $30 → original price = $100. Or: Original Price = Savings ÷ Discount% = $30 ÷ 0.30 = $100. Both methods work.

**What is a good discount percentage?**
Context-dependent. In retail apparel, 20–30% is a standard promotional discount; 40–50% is a significant clearance sale; 60%+ usually indicates the original price was inflated or the item is heavily overstocked. In B2B settings, trade discounts of 30–50% off list price are common for distributors and volume buyers. For negotiating with suppliers, typical starting ask is 5–20% depending on order volume.

**Why do stores show "% savings" instead of "$ savings"?**
Percentage savings appear larger on high-priced items ("Save 25%!" sounds dramatic on a $2,000 refrigerator) and dollar savings appear larger on lower-priced items ("Save $1.50!" on a $6 item sounds better than "Save 25%"). Marketing teams choose the representation that appears most compelling. Always calculate the actual dollar savings when making purchasing decisions — that is the real impact on your wallet.

**How does a trade discount differ from a cash discount?**
A trade discount is a reduction from the list price given to channel partners (wholesalers, retailers) as a standard pricing arrangement — it is factored into the invoice. A cash discount is an incentive for early payment (e.g., "2/10 net 30" means 2% off if paid within 10 days, otherwise full amount due in 30 days). Trade discounts affect product pricing; cash discounts affect payment timing.
