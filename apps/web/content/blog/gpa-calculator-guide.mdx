## What Is GPA?

GPA, or Grade Point Average, is a numerical summary of a student's academic performance expressed on a standardized scale — most commonly the 4.0 scale used by US colleges and universities. It is calculated by converting letter grades to grade points (A = 4.0, B = 3.0, C = 2.0, etc.), weighting each course by its credit hours, and dividing the sum of weighted grade points by the total credit hours attempted.

The credit-hour weighting is important: a grade in a 4-credit course has more impact on your GPA than the same grade in a 1-credit course. This means that performing well in high-credit core courses matters more than in low-credit electives, and that a poor grade in a major course is harder to offset than a poor grade in a minor requirement.

GPA appears on every academic transcript and influences a wide range of decisions. Colleges use it to determine academic standing and eligibility for honors programs. Scholarship committees use minimum GPA thresholds. Graduate schools — medical, law, business, and academic programs — use undergraduate GPA as a primary screening criterion. Some employers, particularly for entry-level roles, request GPA on resumes and filter applicants below a certain threshold.

Understanding how GPA is calculated lets you work backward: if you know your current cumulative GPA and want to reach a target by the end of the semester, you can calculate the exact semester GPA you need to get there. A GPA calculator handles this reverse calculation instantly.

## The GPA Formula

```
GPA = Sum of (Grade Points × Credit Hours) / Sum of Credit Hours
```

**Standard 4.0 scale grade point values:**

| Letter Grade | Grade Points |
|-------------|-------------|
| A+ | 4.0 |
| A | 4.0 |
| A− | 3.7 |
| B+ | 3.3 |
| B | 3.0 |
| B− | 2.7 |
| C+ | 2.3 |
| C | 2.0 |
| C− | 1.7 |
| D+ | 1.3 |
| D | 1.0 |
| D− | 0.7 |
| F | 0.0 |

*Note: Some schools do not use +/− grades or use slightly different point values.*

**Worked example — full semester:**

| Course | Grade | Credits | Quality Points |
|--------|-------|---------|----------------|
| Math | B+ | 4 | 3.3 × 4 = 13.2 |
| English | A | 3 | 4.0 × 3 = 12.0 |
| History | B | 3 | 3.0 × 3 = 9.0 |
| Science | C+ | 4 | 2.3 × 4 = 9.2 |
| **Total** | | **14** | **43.4** |

```
Semester GPA = 43.4 / 14 = 3.10
```

## How to Calculate the GPA You Need

If you want to raise your GPA, calculate the required semester GPA:

```
Required Semester GPA = (Target GPA × (Old Credits + New Credits) − Old GPA × Old Credits) / New Credits
```

**Example:**
- Current GPA: 2.80 over 60 credits
- Target GPA: 3.00
- Next semester: 15 credits

```
Required = (3.00 × (60 + 15) − 2.80 × 60) / 15
         = (3.00 × 75 − 168) / 15
         = (225 − 168) / 15
         = 57 / 15
         = 3.80 GPA needed this semester
```

To raise a 2.80 GPA to 3.00 in one semester of 15 credits, you need approximately a 3.80 semester GPA — mostly A− and A grades. This shows why raising a cumulative GPA significantly is difficult once you have many credits completed: each new semester represents a smaller fraction of the total.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What GPA is needed for graduate school?**
Minimum requirements vary by program. Most graduate programs require a minimum 3.0 GPA for admission; competitive programs (medical school, law school, top MBA programs) look for 3.5–4.0 GPA. However, GPA is one factor — test scores (MCAT, LSAT, GMAT, GRE), research experience, recommendations, and personal statements also matter significantly. A 3.2 GPA with exceptional research experience may outweigh a 3.7 GPA with no research in some graduate science programs.

**What is the difference between GPA and weighted GPA?**
A standard (unweighted) GPA uses the same 4.0 scale for all classes. A weighted GPA assigns higher grade point values to more difficult courses: Honors courses may have a 0.5-point bonus; AP/IB courses may have a 1.0-point bonus. A student earning a B (3.0) in AP Chemistry might receive 4.0 weighted grade points. Weighted GPAs can exceed 4.0 (maximum typically 5.0 or 5.3). Colleges recalculate GPAs on a standardized scale when comparing applicants from different schools.

**Does retaking a course improve GPA?**
It depends on the school's policy. Under academic forgiveness or grade replacement, the new grade replaces the old one in GPA calculations (though both may appear on the transcript). Under grade averaging, both attempts are included in the GPA calculation. Most schools apply one policy consistently. If your school has grade replacement, retaking a failed or poor-grade course can significantly improve GPA — especially for high-credit courses.

**How much does one F hurt your GPA?**
A failing grade (0.0) in a 3-credit course: if you previously had a 3.40 GPA over 45 credits, adding the F: (3.40 × 45 + 0.0 × 3) / 48 = 153 / 48 = 3.19 GPA. A 3-credit F drops a 3.40 GPA by 0.21 points. A 4-credit F would drop it by 0.27 points. Recovering from an F requires multiple semesters of above-average grades.

**What is a good GPA?**
Context-dependent. For most undergraduate programs: 3.5–4.0 is excellent, 3.0–3.5 is good, 2.7–3.0 is satisfactory, below 2.5 may affect standing and opportunities. Academic standing usually requires at least a 2.0 (C average). For competitive internships and graduate programs, 3.5+ is typically advantageous.
