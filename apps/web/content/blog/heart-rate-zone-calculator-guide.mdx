Your maximum heart rate (MHR) determines your five training zones. The standard formula: MHR = 220 − age. A 35-year-old has an estimated MHR of 185 bpm. Training zones are calculated as percentages of MHR: Zone 1 (50–60%), Zone 2 (60–70%), Zone 3 (70–80%), Zone 4 (80–90%), Zone 5 (90–100%). Each zone produces different physiological adaptations and serves different training purposes.

Understanding heart rate zones transforms cardio training from "working out" to "training with purpose." Zone 2 training (60–70% MHR) builds aerobic base and fat-burning efficiency — perfect for long, sustainable endurance. Zone 4 training (80–90% MHR) builds lactate threshold and cardiovascular capacity — the zone that makes you fitter faster. Staying in the wrong zone for your goal is why many people do hours of cardio with disappointing results.

## Maximum Heart Rate Formulas

**Standard formula:**
```
MHR = 220 − age
```

**Tanaka formula (more accurate for older adults):**
```
MHR = 208 − (0.7 × age)
```

**Comparison:**

| Age | 220 − age | Tanaka | Difference |
|-----|----------|--------|------------|
| 25 | 195 bpm | 190 bpm | 5 bpm |
| 40 | 180 bpm | 180 bpm | 0 bpm |
| 55 | 165 bpm | 170 bpm | 5 bpm |
| 70 | 150 bpm | 159 bpm | 9 bpm |

The Tanaka formula gives higher estimates for older adults, which aligns better with observed maximum heart rates. For general zone training, both are adequate estimates.

## The Five Heart Rate Training Zones

**Zone 1: Active Recovery (50–60% MHR)**
- Purpose: Recovery, warm-up, cool-down
- Feel: Very easy; can have a full conversation
- Benefit: Promotes circulation and recovery without adding training stress
- Example: Leisurely walk

**Zone 2: Aerobic Base (60–70% MHR)**
- Purpose: Building aerobic endurance and fat oxidation capacity
- Feel: Comfortable; conversational effort
- Benefit: Trains mitochondria, improves fat burning efficiency, builds aerobic base
- Example: Easy run, comfortable cycling, brisk walk
- Note: This is the "magic zone" that most people neglect — they go too hard to be in Zone 2 but not hard enough for Zone 4, ending up in unproductive Zone 3

**Zone 3: Aerobic Tempo (70–80% MHR)**
- Purpose: Improving aerobic capacity
- Feel: Moderately hard; can speak in short sentences
- Benefit: Improves cardiovascular capacity and endurance
- Example: Moderate run, cycling at tempo pace

**Zone 4: Lactate Threshold (80–90% MHR)**
- Purpose: Raising lactate threshold, improving performance
- Feel: Hard; one-word answers only
- Benefit: Most efficient zone for improving race pace and VO2max
- Example: Race pace runs, interval training, HIIT work intervals
- Warning: Cannot be sustained continuously for more than 20–40 minutes; requires planned recovery

**Zone 5: Maximum Effort (90–100% MHR)**
- Purpose: Maximum power and speed development
- Feel: Maximum effort; cannot speak
- Benefit: Neuromuscular power, speed, final kick in races
- Example: Sprint intervals, all-out efforts lasting 10–60 seconds
- Recovery required: 48–72 hours between Zone 5 sessions

**Worked example — 40-year-old, resting HR 60 bpm:**

MHR (Tanaka) = 208 − (0.7 × 40) = 180 bpm
Heart Rate Reserve (HRR) = 180 − 60 = 120 bpm (Karvonen method)

| Zone | % of MHR | BPM Range (220−age) | Karvonen (more accurate) |
|------|----------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Zone 1 | 50–60% | 90–108 bpm | 120–132 bpm |
| Zone 2 | 60–70% | 108–126 bpm | 132–144 bpm |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | 126–144 bpm | 144–156 bpm |
| Zone 4 | 80–90% | 144–162 bpm | 156–168 bpm |
| Zone 5 | 90–100% | 162–180 bpm | 168–180 bpm |

The Karvonen method shifts all zones upward because it accounts for resting heart rate — a person with a lower resting HR can train at a higher absolute heart rate before reaching a given relative intensity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the "fat burning zone" and is it real?**
The fat-burning zone (Zone 2, 60–70% MHR) is real but often misunderstood. At lower intensities, the body relies more on fat as fuel (as a percentage of total calories burned). But total fat burned depends on both the percentage AND total calorie expenditure. Higher-intensity exercise burns fewer fat calories per minute as a percentage but far more total calories — resulting in more total fat oxidized per hour of exercise. For overall fat loss, total caloric deficit matters most; zone training matters most for athletic performance development.

**How do I measure heart rate during exercise without a monitor?**
The "talk test" is a surprisingly accurate zone indicator. Zone 1–2: full conversation possible. Zone 3: short sentences only. Zone 4: single-word answers. Zone 5: cannot speak. You can also use perceived exertion (RPE scale 1–10): Zone 2 ≈ RPE 3–4, Zone 4 ≈ RPE 7–8. For serious training, a chest strap heart rate monitor is far more accurate than wrist-based optical sensors, especially during high-intensity efforts.

**How long should I spend in each zone per week?**
The 80/20 principle (popularized by Matt Fitzgerald based on elite athlete research): approximately 80% of training time in Zone 1–2 (easy), 20% in Zone 3–5 (hard). Most recreational athletes get this backwards — they train at Zone 3 (moderately hard) for most workouts, which is too hard for easy adaptations and not hard enough for performance gains. True Zone 2 training feels deceptively easy to most people.

**Can I have a naturally high or low maximum heart rate?**
Yes — maximum heart rate is largely genetically determined and does not meaningfully change with fitness level (unlike resting heart rate, which decreases with improved fitness). A highly fit athlete and an unfit person of the same age can have identical maximum heart rates. What changes with fitness is the heart rate you can sustain at a given effort level — trained athletes can sustain higher percentages of MHR for longer periods.

**Does caffeine affect heart rate during exercise?**
Caffeine raises resting and exercise heart rate by approximately 3–7 bpm. This means your actual training zone percentages will correspond to slightly higher BPM than calculated when caffeinated. If you always train caffeinated, this effect is consistent and typically does not cause problems. If heart rate-based training is critical (e.g., you are doing strict Zone 2 training), account for the caffeine effect by adjusting zone targets upward by 5 bpm on training days when caffeinated.
