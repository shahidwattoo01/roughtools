Your estimated due date (EDD) is calculated using Naegele's Rule: add 280 days (40 weeks) to the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP). Alternatively, add one year and seven days to the LMP date, then subtract three months. If your last period started on March 15, your due date is approximately December 20. Only 5% of babies are born on their exact due date — most arrive within 2 weeks before or after.

The due date is one of the first questions in every pregnancy, and for good reason — it determines when prenatal appointments are scheduled, when maternal leave begins, when to prepare the nursery, and at what point labor induction might be considered. Understanding how it is calculated helps you interpret the numbers your healthcare provider gives you and track pregnancy progress week by week.

## The Due Date Formula

**Naegele's Rule:**
```
EDD = LMP + 280 days (40 weeks)
```

Or equivalently:
```
EDD = LMP + 1 year + 7 days − 3 months
```

**Worked example:**
- LMP: January 10
- Add 280 days: October 17
- Or: January 10 + 1 year = January 10 next year − 3 months = October 10 + 7 days = October 17 ✓

**Adjusting for cycle length:**
If your average cycle is not 28 days, adjust the due date:
```
Adjustment (days) = Cycle Length − 28
EDD = Standard EDD + Adjustment
```

If your cycle is 35 days: add 7 days to the standard EDD.
If your cycle is 21 days: subtract 7 days from the standard EDD.

**Gestational age vs. fetal age:** Gestational age counts from the LMP date — the first day of the last period, which is typically 2 weeks before conception. Fetal age counts from conception. This is why pregnancy is counted as 40 weeks from LMP even though actual conception is approximately 38 weeks before birth. Healthcare providers use gestational age; pregnancy apps and calculators all reference gestational age.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How accurate is the due date calculated from LMP?**
Due dates from LMP are estimates — only about 4–5% of babies are born on their exact due date. About 80% of births occur within 2 weeks before or after the EDD. Ultrasound dating in the first trimester (ideally between 8–13 weeks, measuring crown-rump length) is more accurate than LMP dating, with a margin of error of ±5–7 days. If LMP and early ultrasound differ by more than 5–7 days, providers typically update the due date to match the ultrasound.

**What is considered early, full term, and late term?**
Premature: before 37 weeks. Early term: 37–38 weeks 6 days. Full term: 39–40 weeks 6 days. Late term: 41–41 weeks 6 days. Post term: 42+ weeks. The "full term" definition was refined in 2013 — previously 37 weeks was considered full term, but research showed that 37–38 week babies have more complications than 39–40 week babies. Most providers now aim to avoid elective induction or C-section before 39 weeks without medical indication.

**When should I have my first prenatal appointment?**
Most providers recommend the first prenatal visit between weeks 8–12. Earlier visits (before 8 weeks) are sometimes appropriate to confirm pregnancy, check for ectopic pregnancy symptoms, or for high-risk pregnancies. The first appointment typically includes: due date confirmation, medical history review, blood work (blood type, Rh factor, complete blood count, STI screening, immunity tests), urine testing, and sometimes the first ultrasound (dating scan).

**Can the due date be wrong based on ultrasound?**
Ultrasound is very accurate in the first trimester for dating (±5–7 days) but becomes less accurate as pregnancy progresses. In the second trimester, ultrasound can be off by ±2 weeks; in the third trimester, ±3 weeks. This is because fetal size differences between pregnancies become more pronounced as growth varies by genetics and nutrition. The first-trimester ultrasound measurement is the gold standard for due date accuracy when available.

**What happens if my pregnancy goes past the due date?**
Going past 40 weeks is very common — approximately 10–15% of pregnancies reach 42 weeks or beyond. At 41 weeks, most providers begin discussing induction. After 42 weeks, the placenta may begin to function less efficiently, and induction or C-section is typically recommended. During the post-date period, providers typically increase monitoring: non-stress tests, biophysical profiles, and amniotic fluid assessments to ensure the baby remains healthy.
