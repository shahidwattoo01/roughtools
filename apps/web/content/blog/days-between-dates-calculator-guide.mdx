## How the Calculation Works

The simplest method for dates within a year: count days remaining in the starting month + days in each full intervening month + days elapsed in the ending month.

For multi-year spans, the formula becomes:
```
Days = (End Year − Start Year) × 365 + Extra days for leap years + Day-of-year difference
```

For exact precision, most software uses Julian Day Numbers (a continuous count of days since a reference point), then subtracts.

## Counting Working Days vs. Calendar Days

**Calendar days** count every day including weekends and holidays.

**Working days** (business days) count only Monday–Friday, excluding weekends. Some calculations also need to exclude public holidays:

```
Working Days = Total Calendar Days − Weekend Days − Public Holidays
```

Where Weekend Days = number of Saturdays and Sundays in the range. For a 30-day period: approximately 30 − (30/7 × 2) ≈ 21.6 working days on average, or exactly count from a calendar.

**When working days matter:**
- Legal response deadlines ("answer within 30 business days")
- Payment terms ("net 30" in invoicing means 30 calendar days, but "30 business days" means ~6 weeks)
- Project timelines
- Employment notice periods
- Stock settlement (T+1 or T+2 settlement in business days)

## Common Date Calculation Scenarios

**Countdown to event:**
Days until a future event: calculate from today to the event date. Your age at future event: age + (days until event / 365.25).

**"How long ago" calculations:**
Days since a past event: event date to today. Useful for tracking "days sober," "days since last incident," project elapsed time.

**Contract and notice periods:**
Employment contracts often specify notice periods (30 days, 90 days). The period begins the day after the notice is given. Always check: are weekends included? Most employment notice periods are calendar days. Lease termination notices are usually calendar days.

**Payment terms:**
"Net 30" = payment due 30 calendar days from invoice date. "30/10, Net 30" = 2% discount if paid within 10 days, full amount due in 30 days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the date calculation include both the start and end date?**
This depends on the context and convention. Most "days between" calculations exclude the start date and include the end date (so "from Monday to Friday" = 4 days). Some applications include both (Monday to Friday = 5 days, including both ends). Legal periods often include both endpoints. The RoughTools calculator shows both conventions.

**How do I calculate dates across leap years?**
A leap year occurs every 4 years (1996, 2000, 2004...) with exceptions: years divisible by 100 are NOT leap years unless also divisible by 400. So 1900 was not a leap year; 2000 was. The date calculator automatically handles leap years in the calculation, adding 366 days for each leap year in the range instead of 365.

**How are payment due dates calculated for invoices?**
Invoice date + payment terms = due date. Net 30 from January 15: February 14 (January has 31 days: 31−15=16 remaining days in January + 14 days in February = 30 days). Most invoicing software adds exactly 30 calendar days to the invoice date. If the due date falls on a weekend or holiday, it typically shifts to the next business day.

**How do I count "days since" a historical date?**
Enter the historical date as the start date and today as the end date. The calculator gives total days elapsed. Divide by 365.25 to convert to approximate years. Historical examples: the Apollo 11 moon landing (July 20, 1969) was 20,782 days before June 14, 2026 — almost 57 years ago.

**Why do some months have different numbers of days?**
The modern Gregorian calendar has months of 28–31 days, a legacy of Roman calendar reforms. Originally, March was the first month (which is why September, October, November, December mean 7th, 8th, 9th, 10th month in Latin). Julius Caesar added January and February; Augustus Caesar adjusted day counts. February is short because days were taken from it to add to July (Julius) and August (Augustus), though this historical story is partially myth.
