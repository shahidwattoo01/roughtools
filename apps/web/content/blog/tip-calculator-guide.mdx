To calculate a tip, multiply the pre-tax bill by the tip percentage. A $65 restaurant bill with an 18% tip: $65 × 0.18 = $11.70 tip. Total bill: $65 + $11.70 = $76.70. To split among 4 people: $76.70 ÷ 4 = $19.18 each. For a quick mental estimate of 20%: move the decimal one place left ($65 → $6.50) and double it ($13.00).

Tipping norms vary significantly by service type, country, and region. What is expected at a sit-down restaurant is different from what is appropriate at a coffee counter, and US tipping culture is very different from tipping in Japan or the UK. Knowing the norms helps you tip appropriately without either undertipping service workers who depend on gratuities or overtipping in situations where it is not expected.

## Mental Math Shortcuts

**20% tip (most common):**
```
Move decimal one left = 10%
Double it = 20%
$82 bill → $8.20 × 2 = $16.40 tip
```

**15% tip:**
```
10% + half of 10% = 15%
$82 → $8.20 + $4.10 = $12.30 tip
```

**For any percentage:**
```
Bill × (Tip% / 100) = Tip Amount
$82 × 0.18 = $14.76
```

**Splitting evenly:**
```
(Bill + Tip) ÷ Number of People = Per Person Amount
($82 + $16.40) ÷ 6 = $16.40 each
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I tip on the pre-tax or total (with tax) amount?**
Strictly speaking, the customary tip is calculated on the pre-tax amount — you should not tip on the tax the restaurant collects for the government. In practice, most people tip on the total including tax, which increases the effective tip percentage by a few percent. On a $60 pre-tax bill with 9% tax ($65.40 total), tipping 20% on the total ($13.08) versus on the pre-tax ($12.00) is a difference of $1.08 — not significant enough to overthink.

**Is it rude not to tip in the US?**
In the US, tipping at full-service restaurants is considered an obligation, not optional. Servers are legally paid a "tipped minimum wage" ($2.13/hour federally, though many states are higher) under the assumption that tips will bring total compensation to at least minimum wage. Not tipping effectively means the server earned below minimum wage for your table. For genuinely poor service, a reduced tip (10%) communicates dissatisfaction while acknowledging the baseline expectation. For non-tip situations (counter service, fast food), tips are appreciated but truly optional.

**How do tip jars at coffee counters work?**
Counter service tip jars (and their digital equivalents on touchscreen payment terminals) are not subject to the same social obligation as full-service restaurant tips. The people behind a coffee counter are typically paid at least minimum wage and sometimes more. However, if the barista spends time on a complex drink or provides notably friendly service, a $1 tip is a generous gesture. The preset 20–25% digital tip options on counter-service screens are more than most people leave — do not feel pressured by the screen.

**How much do servers actually make from tips?**
In the US, servers at full-service restaurants typically earn $15–50/hour total (tips plus tipped minimum wage) depending on the restaurant's price point and volume. At a busy upscale restaurant, experienced servers can earn $50–100+/hour on busy weekend evenings. At a casual chain, $10–20/hour is more typical. Tips are taxable income; servers are required to report tips and pay income tax on them, though the cash nature of tips historically led to underreporting. Digital payment has increased tip transparency and tax compliance.

**What is a service charge and is it different from a tip?**
Some restaurants — particularly in tourist areas, with large parties (often 6+ people), or at high-end establishments — add an automatic service charge (usually 18–22%) to the bill. This is different from a tip: it is included in your bill total, not optional, and may not go entirely to your server (some restaurants pool service charges or apply them to various operating costs). If you see an automatic service charge, you are generally not expected to add additional tip unless service was exceptional.
