Net Present Value (NPV) calculates the current worth of future cash flows, discounted by a required rate of return. NPV = Sum of [Cash Flow_t / (1 + r)^t] − Initial Investment, where r = discount rate and t = time period. If an investment of $10,000 returns $4,000, $4,000, and $4,000 over 3 years at a 10% discount rate: NPV = $4,000/1.1 + $4,000/1.21 + $4,000/1.331 − $10,000 = $3,636 + $3,306 + $3,005 − $10,000 = **−$53**. Negative NPV: the investment does not meet the required return.

NPV is the gold standard method for evaluating investments, capital expenditures, business projects, and financial decisions. It answers the fundamental question: "Is this investment worth more than it costs, accounting for the time value of money?" A positive NPV means the investment creates value; a negative NPV destroys value relative to your required return.

## The NPV Formula

```
NPV = Σ [CF_t / (1 + r)^t] - I₀

Where:
CF_t = Cash flow in period t
r    = Discount rate (required rate of return)
t    = Time period (year 1, 2, 3...)
I₀   = Initial investment (negative cash flow at time 0)
```

**Decision rule:**
- NPV > 0: Accept the investment (creates value above required return)
- NPV = 0: Indifferent (exactly meets required return)
- NPV < 0: Reject the investment (doesn't meet required return)
- When comparing mutually exclusive projects: choose the higher NPV

## Worked Example: Business Equipment Purchase

Should a business invest $50,000 in new equipment that generates $15,000 in additional annual cash flows for 5 years, then has a salvage value of $5,000? Required return: 12%.

| Year | Cash Flow | Discount Factor (12%) | Present Value |
|------|-----------|----------------------|--------------|
| 0 (Investment) | −$50,000 | 1.000 | −$50,000 |
| 1 | $15,000 | 1/1.12 = 0.893 | $13,393 |
| 2 | $15,000 | 1/1.2544 = 0.797 | $11,958 |
| 3 | $15,000 | 1/1.4049 = 0.712 | $10,677 |
| 4 | $15,000 | 1/1.5735 = 0.636 | $9,533 |
| 5 | $15,000 + $5,000 = $20,000 | 1/1.7623 = 0.567 | $11,348 |
| **NPV** | | | **$6,909** |

NPV = +$6,909 → **Accept the investment** (creates $6,909 in value above the 12% hurdle rate)

## Choosing the Discount Rate

The discount rate represents the opportunity cost of capital — what return you could earn on the next best alternative investment:

**For businesses:**
- WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital): blend of cost of debt and equity
- Typical range: 8–15% for established companies; 15–25% for riskier ventures
- Project risk premium: riskier projects use higher discount rates

**For personal finance:**
- Conservative personal finance decisions: 4–6% (compare to savings account returns)
- Compare to investment alternatives: 7–10% (compare to expected stock market return)
- Credit card debt comparison: 20–25% (the debt's interest rate is the hurdle rate)

## NPV vs. IRR

**Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** is the discount rate that makes NPV = 0. It tells you the actual annual return of an investment.

Using the equipment example above, the IRR is approximately 18.3% (the rate at which NPV = 0). Since IRR (18.3%) > Required Return (12%), this confirms the investment is worthwhile — same conclusion as positive NPV.

**When they disagree (mutually exclusive projects):**
NPV is the more reliable decision criterion when choosing between projects because it measures absolute value created, not just percentage return. A $1 investment with 200% IRR might create less value than a $1,000,000 investment with 15% IRR if capital is sufficient.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the time value of money and why does it matter for NPV?**
The time value of money states that $1 today is worth more than $1 in the future because today's dollar can be invested to earn returns. NPV "discounts" future cash flows back to their present value to make them comparable to today's investment. Without discounting, you could compare a $100 investment that returns $110 in 30 years (bad deal) to one returning $110 in 1 year (good deal) — NPV correctly shows the 30-year investment has negative NPV while the 1-year investment has positive NPV.

**How do I calculate NPV in Excel or Google Sheets?**
Excel: `=NPV(rate, value1, value2, ...) + initial_investment` where the NPV function discounts future cash flows only (not the initial investment at time 0). Example: `=NPV(0.10, B2:B6) + B1` where B1 is the initial investment as a negative number and B2:B6 are years 1–5 cash flows. Google Sheets uses the same syntax. Note: the Excel NPV function assumes end-of-period cash flows — if flows occur at the beginning of periods, use XNPV instead.

**Can NPV be used for personal financial decisions?**
Yes — NPV applies to any decision involving spending money now for future benefits: Should you buy or lease a car? (Compare NPVs of both options.) Is a graduate degree worth it? (Initial tuition cost vs. higher future salary, discounted at your opportunity cost.) Should you prepay your mortgage? (Compare after-tax return on the prepayment vs. investing that money.) Should you buy or rent a home? (Requires projecting purchase, appreciation, rental equivalent, and all associated costs.)

**What does a $0 NPV investment mean?**
An NPV of exactly $0 means the investment earns precisely the required rate of return — no more, no less. For businesses, this means the project earns exactly the cost of capital (WACC). The IRR equals the discount rate. Whether to accept a zero-NPV project depends on strategic factors (market entry, capability building, competitive necessity) that aren't captured in the cash flow model. Most capital budgeting frameworks require NPV > 0 to accept a project, rejecting zero-NPV investments in favor of alternatives.

**What are the limitations of NPV analysis?**
NPV is powerful but has limitations: cash flow estimates are inherently uncertain; the discount rate selection is subjective; NPV ignores optionality (the value of future flexibility); doesn't easily capture qualitative strategic benefits; assumes cash flows can be reinvested at the discount rate (which may not be possible). For major investments, NPV analysis should be combined with scenario analysis (best/base/worst case), sensitivity analysis (which assumptions drive the outcome most), and qualitative strategic assessment.
