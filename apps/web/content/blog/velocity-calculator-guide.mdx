## What Is a Velocity Calculator?

A velocity calculator solves for any unknown in the speed-distance-time relationship: given any two of the three values, it calculates the third. It also handles unit conversions — switching between mph, km/h, m/s, and other speed units — and can solve acceleration problems using the kinematic equations of motion.

Speed and velocity are often used interchangeably in everyday language, but in physics they have a precise distinction. Speed is a scalar — it has magnitude only. "60 mph" is a speed. Velocity is a vector — it has both magnitude and direction. "60 mph heading north" is a velocity. Average speed is total distance divided by total time; average velocity is total displacement (straight-line change in position) divided by time, which can be zero if you return to your starting point.

The three-variable formula (speed = distance ÷ time) is the foundation for hundreds of practical calculations: estimated time of arrival for a road trip, race finish time from a known pace, speed of a falling object, fuel economy at highway speeds, and travel time for aircraft or ships. Any situation where you move a known distance at a known speed — or want to find either from the other — uses this relationship.

Acceleration adds a fourth variable to the mix: the rate at which velocity changes over time. Kinematic equations connect initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, and distance, allowing you to solve problems like "how far does a car travel while braking from 60 mph to 0?" or "what is the speed of a ball after falling 10 meters?"

## Speed, Distance, Time Formula Triangle

```
    Distance
   -----------
  Speed × Time
```

The "magic triangle": cover the value you want to find, and the remaining two values show the formula:
- Cover Distance → Speed × Time
- Cover Speed → Distance ÷ Time
- Cover Time → Distance ÷ Speed

## Speed vs. Velocity: What's the Difference?

**Speed** is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude (size) only. "60 mph" is a speed.

**Velocity** is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude AND direction. "60 mph heading north" is a velocity.

In everyday usage, the terms are interchangeable. In physics, the distinction matters:
- Average speed = total distance / total time (always positive)
- Average velocity = displacement / time (can be zero if you return to start)

A car that drives 5 miles east and 5 miles back west in 30 minutes has: average speed = 10 miles / 0.5 hr = 20 mph, but average velocity = 0 mph (displacement = 0).

## Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a = (Final Velocity − Initial Velocity) / Time

```
a = (v_f - v_i) / t

Final velocity: v_f = v_i + a×t
Distance: d = v_i×t + ½×a×t²
Velocity-distance: v_f² = v_i² + 2×a×d
```

**Example — 0-60 mph in 4 seconds:**
- v_i = 0 mph = 0 m/s
- v_f = 60 mph = 26.82 m/s
- t = 4 seconds
- Acceleration = (26.82 − 0) / 4 = **6.7 m/s²** (about 0.68 g-force)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I calculate average speed for a trip with multiple segments?**
Average speed = Total Distance ÷ Total Time (not the average of the speeds). Example: driving 60 miles at 30 mph (takes 2 hours) then 60 miles at 60 mph (takes 1 hour). Total distance = 120 miles. Total time = 3 hours. Average speed = 120/3 = **40 mph** (not the arithmetic average of 45 mph). The arithmetic average of speeds is only equal to true average speed when equal distances are covered at each speed.

**What is terminal velocity?**
Terminal velocity is the constant speed an object reaches when drag force (air resistance) equals gravitational force, resulting in zero net force and zero acceleration. The object stops accelerating and falls at constant speed. For a human in free fall, terminal velocity is approximately 120 mph (53 m/s) in the standard belly-down position, or 180+ mph in a head-down dive. A skydiver's terminal velocity determines when they reach peak speed before parachute deployment.

**How fast is the speed of sound and what is a sonic boom?**
The speed of sound at sea level at 68°F (20°C) is approximately 343 m/s (767 mph, Mach 1). It decreases at higher altitudes (colder air). A sonic boom is the shockwave created when an object travels faster than Mach 1 — the pressure waves cannot propagate ahead of the object and pile up into a conical shockwave that observers hear as a sudden loud crack when the cone passes them. Aircraft breaking the sound barrier first achieved in 1947 by Chuck Yeager in the Bell X-1 (Mach 1.06).

**What is the difference between instantaneous and average velocity?**
Average velocity = total displacement / total time — the single constant velocity that would produce the same displacement in the same time. Instantaneous velocity = velocity at a specific instant in time = the limit of average velocity as the time interval approaches zero = the derivative of position with respect to time (dx/dt in calculus). A speedometer shows instantaneous speed. Average speed for a road trip is calculated after the trip is complete.

**How does velocity relate to momentum and kinetic energy?**
Momentum = mass × velocity (p = mv). Kinetic energy = ½ × mass × velocity² (KE = ½mv²). Doubling velocity doubles momentum but quadruples kinetic energy. This is why crash severity increases dramatically with speed: a car going 60 mph in a crash has 4× the kinetic energy of one going 30 mph, requiring the crumple zones to absorb far more energy. Highway crashes are so much more deadly than city crashes largely because KE scales with velocity squared.
