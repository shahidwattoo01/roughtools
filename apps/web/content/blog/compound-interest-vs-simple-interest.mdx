**Compound interest** grows on itself — you earn interest on your interest. **Simple interest** only grows on the original amount you deposited. That one difference is why two people who invest the same $8,500 can end up with $23,375 versus $46,133 after 25 years.

The gap seems abstract until you see the numbers. According to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), investors who understand compounding consistently make better savings and investment decisions than those who do not. Most savings accounts, index funds, and retirement accounts use compound interest. Most personal loans and short-term financing use simple interest. Knowing which applies to your money tells you exactly how fast it is growing — or costing you.

## The Compound Interest and Simple Interest Formulas

Both formulas start with the same inputs but diverge in how they treat earned interest.

**Simple Interest:**
```
A = P × (1 + r × t)
Interest earned = P × r × t
```

**Compound Interest:**
```
A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)
Interest earned = A - P
```

Where:
- **A** — the final amount (principal + all interest earned)
- **P** — the **principal**, meaning the original amount deposited or invested
- **r** — the annual interest rate in decimal form (7% = 0.07)
- **t** — time in years
- **n** — the number of times interest compounds per year (12 = monthly, 4 = quarterly, 1 = annually)

### Worked example: the same $8,500 over 25 years at 7%

**Simple interest:**
```
A = 8,500 × (1 + 0.07 × 25)
A = 8,500 × (1 + 1.75)
A = 8,500 × 2.75
A = $23,375
Interest earned: $14,875
```

**Compound interest (compounded annually, n = 1):**
```
A = 8,500 × (1 + 0.07/1)^(1 × 25)
A = 8,500 × (1.07)^25
A = 8,500 × 5.4274
A = $46,133
Interest earned: $37,633
```

The compound interest account earned **$22,758 more** than the simple interest account — on the exact same starting amount, same rate, same time period. That extra $22,758 came entirely from earning interest on previously earned interest, a process called **compounding**.

In practice, most savings accounts and investment accounts compound monthly or daily, which produces even higher returns than annual compounding. The $46,133 figure above is actually conservative.

## How to Compare Compound vs Simple Interest Step by Step

1. **Gather your four inputs.** You need your principal (starting amount), annual interest rate, time in years, and compounding frequency. For simple interest, compounding frequency is irrelevant — you only need principal, rate, and time. For compound interest, knowing whether it compounds monthly, quarterly, or annually significantly changes the result.

2. **Convert the interest rate to decimal.** Divide the percentage by 100. A 6.5% rate becomes 0.065. This step is required for both formulas. Using 6.5 instead of 0.065 inflates the result by a factor of 100 — a common error that produces obviously wrong answers.

3. **Calculate simple interest first.** Multiply principal × rate × time, then add the principal. On $8,500 at 7% for 25 years: 8,500 × 0.07 × 25 = $14,875 in interest, giving $23,375 total. This is your baseline — the minimum you would earn if interest never compounded.

4. **Calculate compound interest.** Divide the annual rate by n (compounding periods per year), add 1, raise it to the power of n × t, then multiply by the principal. For monthly compounding (n = 12): A = 8,500 × (1 + 0.07/12)^(12×25) = 8,500 × (1.005833)^300 = 8,500 × 5.7844 = $49,168. Monthly compounding earns an additional $3,035 over annual compounding.

5. **Calculate the difference.** Subtract the simple interest total from the compound interest total. This gap is the pure value of compounding — money earned not from your original deposit, but from interest earning interest over time. The longer the time horizon, the larger this gap becomes.

6. **Verify the result makes sense.** A rough sanity check: at 7% annual simple interest, money roughly doubles in about 14 years. At 7% compound interest, money doubles in about 10 years (using the Rule of 72: 72 ÷ 7 = 10.3 years). If your compound result shows less than your simple result, you have made an error in the exponent calculation.

## Which Earns More: Compound or Simple Interest Over Time?

Compound interest always earns more than simple interest over time, assuming the same rate and principal. The longer the time period, the wider the gap becomes.

Here is the same $8,500 at 7% shown across different time periods:

| Time period | Simple interest total | Compound interest total (annual) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 years | $11,475 | $11,920 | $445 |
| 10 years | $14,450 | $16,718 | $2,268 |
| 20 years | $20,400 | $32,878 | $12,478 |
| 30 years | $26,350 | $64,723 | $38,373 |
| 40 years | $32,300 | $127,327 | $95,027 |

In the first five years, the difference is modest — just $445. By year 40, compounding has produced $95,027 more from the exact same starting deposit. This exponential acceleration is why financial planners emphasize starting early. A 22-year-old who invests $8,500 once and leaves it untouched until age 62 ends up with $127,327. Someone who waits until age 32 ends up with $64,723 — roughly half, from waiting just 10 years.

The difference between compound and simple interest grows slowly at first and then dramatically. The last 10 years of a 40-year timeline generate more wealth than the first 30 years combined. This is the mathematical reason behind the advice to invest early — time is the multiplier that makes compounding powerful.

## How Does Compounding Frequency Affect Your Returns?

More frequent compounding always produces a higher return. The difference between annual and daily compounding is real, though smaller than most people expect for typical deposit amounts.

On the same $8,500 at 7% over 25 years, here is how compounding frequency changes the outcome:

| Compounding frequency | n value | Final amount | Interest earned |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annually | 1 | $46,133 | $37,633 |
| Quarterly | 4 | $48,609 | $40,109 |
| Monthly | 12 | $49,168 | $40,668 |
| Daily | 365 | $49,352 | $40,852 |

The jump from annual to monthly compounding adds $3,035 over 25 years on an $8,500 deposit. The jump from monthly to daily adds only $184 more. This is why high-yield savings accounts that advertise daily compounding are genuinely better than annual-compounding accounts — but the gap between monthly and daily is small enough that rate differences matter far more than compounding frequency when comparing accounts.

The key concept here is the **effective annual rate (EAR)** — also called the annual percentage yield (APY). APY converts any compounding frequency into an equivalent annual rate for easy comparison. A 7% rate compounded monthly has an APY of 7.23%. Banks are required by law to disclose APY, so when comparing savings accounts, APY is the single most useful number — it already accounts for compounding frequency.

## Is Compound Interest Good or Bad When You Are Borrowing?

Compound interest works against you when you are the borrower. Every dollar of interest you do not pay gets added to your balance and starts accruing interest of its own.

Credit cards are the clearest example. A $4,200 credit card balance at 22% APR, paid only the minimum each month, does not disappear in a few years — it can take over a decade to pay off and cost more than twice the original balance in interest. That is compound interest working in reverse, against the borrower.

Compare this to a simple interest car loan at the same 22% rate. Simple interest auto loans calculate interest daily on the remaining balance, and every payment directly reduces principal. The total interest cost is predictable and decreasing. Compound interest debt is neither — unpaid balances accelerate.

The type of interest matters:

- **Simple interest:** most auto loans, some personal loans, US savings bonds
- **Compound interest (works for you):** savings accounts, money market accounts, index funds, retirement accounts
- **Compound interest (works against you):** credit cards, most student loans, some mortgages with deferred interest

Before taking any loan, ask explicitly whether interest is simple or compound, and whether any unpaid interest capitalizes (gets added to the principal balance). Capitalized interest is compound interest in disguise — common in student loan deferment periods and some adjustable-rate mortgages.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid When Comparing Compound and Simple Interest

- **Using the interest rate instead of APY to compare accounts.** A savings account at 5.00% compounded monthly has an APY of 5.12%. A CD at 5.05% compounded annually has an APY of 5.05%. The first account earns more despite the lower stated rate. Always compare APY, not the nominal rate.

- **Assuming compound interest always means annually.** When someone says "compound interest," they have not told you the compounding frequency. Annual, monthly, daily, and continuously compounded interest all use the same formula but produce different results. Always ask or check the account disclosures.

- **Forgetting to account for taxes on interest earned.** Interest income in a standard taxable account is taxed as ordinary income each year. A 7% nominal return on a taxable account is not 7% net. In a 22% tax bracket, the after-tax return is closer to 5.46%. In a Roth IRA or 401(k), compounding is tax-sheltered — which is an additional benefit that most compound interest vs simple interest comparisons ignore.

- **Applying the compound interest formula to simple interest loans.** Auto loans that say "6% interest" use a daily simple interest calculation, not the compound formula. Using the compound formula on a simple interest loan dramatically overstates the total interest paid. Verify the loan type before calculating.

- **Underestimating the time variable.** Most people linearly estimate compounding — they expect 20 years to produce roughly twice what 10 years produces. It does not. At 7%, 10 years turns $10,000 into $19,672. Twenty years turns it into $38,697 — nearly double the 10-year result, from the same starting amount. Compounding is exponential; intuitive linear thinking consistently underestimates it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the simplest way to explain compound interest vs simple interest?**
Simple interest pays you only on your original deposit. Compound interest pays you on your original deposit plus on all the interest you have already earned. On a $5,000 deposit at 6% for 10 years, simple interest gives you $8,000. Compound interest (annually) gives you $8,954 — an extra $954 that came entirely from interest earning interest.

**What if I add money regularly instead of investing a lump sum?**
Regular contributions dramatically accelerate compounding. Adding $200/month to an account starting with $8,500 at 7% for 25 years produces approximately $186,000 — compared to $46,133 with no contributions. The [savings calculator](/tools/calculators/savings-calculator) handles this calculation automatically, including variable contribution amounts and compounding frequency.

**What is the difference between APR and APY?**
APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the stated interest rate before compounding. APY (Annual Percentage Yield) is the effective rate after compounding is applied. They are the same only when compounding is annual. For monthly compounding, APY is always higher than APR. Lenders are required to disclose APR; banks are required to disclose APY. Use APY when comparing savings accounts, APR when comparing loan costs.

**How much does $10,000 grow with compound interest over 30 years?**
At 6% compounded annually, $10,000 grows to $57,435 over 30 years. At 8%, it grows to $100,627 — crossing six figures from a single $10,000 deposit. At 10%, it reaches $174,494. The rate makes an enormous difference over long time horizons. Use the [investment calculator](/tools/calculators/investment-calculator) to model any rate, time period, and compounding frequency side by side.

**When should I use simple interest vs compound interest calculations?**
Use simple interest for short-term loans (under 1 year), auto loans, and any debt described as "simple interest." Use compound interest for savings accounts, investment accounts, retirement projections, credit card debt, and any scenario involving reinvested interest. When in doubt, check the account or loan disclosures — they must state the APY or APR and whether interest capitalizes.
